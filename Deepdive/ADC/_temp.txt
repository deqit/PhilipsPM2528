Reverse Engineering the Philips PM2528: A Peek Inside a Vintage RMS Multimeter
The Philips PM2528 is a precision RMS multimeter from the late 1970s to early 1980s—a period when test equipment was transitioning from analog dials to digital displays, and microcontrollers were just beginning to appear in lab gear. Buried inside the steel case of this 5.5-digit instrument is a mix of analog precision, discrete logic, and early firmware-controlled automation. In this post, I’ll take a close look at how the PM2528 works, focusing for now on its dual-slope ADC design.

The goal of this project is documenting 'everything' about this multimeter, and simultaniously talk about the concept used in this device.

A personal aside
I recently acquired this Philips PM2528 RMS multimeter in non-working condition and decided to restore it. Half-functional, no manuals, and full of mystery. Perfect.

There wasn’t a complete service manual available, so I decided to do what any curious engineer would: reverse engineer it.

If you're interested in what I did, take a look in the GitHub repository. It contains annotated schematics (KiCad), a riser card, the relay matrix, and the partially disassembled firmware (Ghidra). It’s all a work in progress, and there are likely some mistakes or gaps. Corrections, contributions, and curiosity are all welcome.

Introduction: A Multimeter with a Microprocessor Soul
Unlike modern instruments that rely on integrated chips, the PM2528 is built with a mixture of analog and logic-level circuits driven by an Intel 8035 microcontroller.

Measurement Capabilities
The PM2528 supports a broad range of measurements for its era, including:

DC Voltage: 200 mV to 2000 V (max 1000 V continuous)

AC Voltage (RMS): 200 mV to 2000 V (600 Vrms max)

AC + DC Voltage: 200 mV to 2000 V (600 Vrms / 900 V peak max)

DC Current: 2 µA to 2000 mA (2500 mA max)

AC + DC Current: 2 µA to 2000 mA (2500 mA max)

Resistance (2-wire): 200 Ω to 2000 MΩ

Resistance (4-wire): 200 Ω to 2000 kΩ

Temperature (via external PT100 sensor): –220 °C to +850 °C

Optional features (not present in my unit) included:

Peak voltage capture

High-frequency AC voltage measurement

System Overview: Four Functional Blocks
The PM2528 is organized into four primary functional blocks, each with clearly defined responsibilities.

1. Analog Front-End

All measurements begin here. The analog section accepts voltage, current, resistance, or temperature inputs and scales them down to normalized voltage levels. These conditioned signals are then routed to the ADC for digitization. The analog input circuits also include protection circuitry and signal routing via relays, depending on the selected function and range.

2. Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)

At the heart of the PM2528 is a dual-slope integrating ADC, a method widely used in precision multimeters of this era for its excellent noise rejection and accuracy. Unlike modern designs that use a single-chip ADC, the PM2528 implements this function across two full circuit boards.

We’ll dive into the ADC in more detail below.

3. Control Logic and CPU

The PM2528 is controlled by an Intel 8035 microcontroller running at 6 MHz. It has 64 bytes of internal RAM and no on-chip ROM. The firmware lives in two external 2 KB EPROMs.

This microcontroller handles sequencing of measurement operations, display updates, user inputs, and communication over GPIB. Inputs to the CPU include front panel controls, GPIB interface, ADC conversion results and external trigger signals. Outputs are sent to a 7-segment display,  LEDs, Relay drivers and some external interfaces.

4. Display and User Interface

The user interacts with the multimeter through a combination of buttons and a display: A 6-digit 7-segment LED array shows readings and units, LEDs (in fact: small lamps) indicate selected modes and functions, and function-keys allow measurement mode selection, ranging, and triggering.

The ADC: Dual-Slope Precision from Discrete Parts
The ADC in the PM2528 is a classic dual-slope integrating converter, ideal for high-precision measurements with good noise immunity. Unlike most modern designs, this ADC is built entirely from discrete components.

A dual-slope ADC operates in two phases:

Ramp-Up (Integration):
The unknown input voltage charges an integrator (op-amp + capacitor) for a fixed period. The output voltage ramps up (or down) linearly.

Ramp-Down (De-integration):
A known reference voltage of opposite polarity is applied. The time required to bring the integrator’s output back to zero is measured by counting clock pulses.

This gives the relationship:

Vin = (Tdown / Tup) × Vref

Since the integration period (Tup) and Vref are known and stable, measuring Tdown gives an accurate representation of the input voltage.

Implementation Details
Performing a measurement requires four components: Expansion boards N20 (ADC Control) and N21 (ADC Core), a 20.000 counts counter  and the CPU.


Board N20 – ADC Control

This board forms the digital control interface between the microcontroller, the ADC core and the counter. Its functions include:

Translating CPU commands into timing signals

Issues control signals like SRUP (Start Ramp Up), AZ (Auto Zero), UP/DN± (Ramp Direction)

Handling feedback from the analog section (e.g., comparator output)

Returning conversion results to the CPU

It also includes logic to divide the clock and count 20,000 pulse blocks using a carry signal (C20000), a technique that reduces the counter width while preserving resolution.

Board N21 – ADC Core

Here lies the analog heart of the converter:

Integrator: An op-amp and capacitor form the core integration circuit

Comparator: Detects when the integrator output crosses zero

Reference Voltage: Provides stable reference for de-integration

Auto-Zeroing: Ensures measurement accuracy by compensating for offset voltages

Signal Input Path: Receives scaled signals from the analog front-end

This board operates largely autonomously, driven by the control signals generated by N20.

A Measurement Cycle in Detail
Let’s walk through a full measurement cycle using logic analyzer captures.

The Clock Source
Timing accuracy is crucial in a dual-slope converter. The CPU provides a 2 MHz clock (labeled CP) used by the control logic. All measurement intervals are derived from this base clock. With a known time base, the measurement reduces to counting pulses.

Phase 1: Ramp-Up (Integration)
The measurement begins when the CPU asserts the SRUP signal (Start Ramp Up).

The ADC Control resets the 20,000-count counter (RC = Reset Counter)

Clock pulses (CPC) begin driving the counter

AZ is released, disabling auto-zeroing and allowing the integrator to start

UP is asserted, directing the ADC Analog to integrate the input voltage

Once the comparator detects voltage, it asserts the COMP signal

The integration period is fixed: 200,000 clock pulses, exactly 100 ms at 2 MHz. During this time, the input voltage charges the integrator’s capacitor linearly.

Interestingly, the COMP signal doesn’t activate immediately. There’s a slight delay, likely due to residual offset or incomplete zeroing at the comparator input. Time to calibrate.




During ramp-up, the counter tracks every CPC pulse. For every 20,000 counts, the carry-out (C20000) toggles. The ADC Control logic uses this to track progress toward the 200,000-pulse goal. Once ten carry pulses have been seen, the ramp-up phase ends.

At this point, the capacitor holds a charge proportional to the input voltage.

Phase 2: Ramp-Down (de-integration)
Now comes the clever part. The integrator is discharged using a fixed reference current, and the time it takes to return to zero is measured.

The UP signal is deasserted
The DN+ (or DN-, depending on input polarity) is asserted to begin ramp-down
The counter is reset again via RC
The CPU counts how many times the counter hits 20,000 using the XC20 signal

This continues until the comparator detects zero crossing, at which point COMP goes low again. The ADC Control:

Halts the counter by stopping the CPC clock
Resets the DN± signal
Re-asserts AZ to begin a new auto-zero cycle
The CPU pulses the SAZ signal to be sure the auto-zero, to make sure the auto-zero cycle starts. Probably to mitigate a failed measurement.

At this point, the measurement is complete.

Now the CPU needs to calculate the input voltage, based on the number of pulses counted during the Ramp-Down phase.

In this example (see the measurement-pane in the image below):

Ramp-up phase = 200.000 counts
Ramp-down phase = 363.666-200.000=163.666 counts (=8x20.000 from XC20 + 3.666 from the counter)
Reference-voltage = 2V (see the schematics in the Github-repository)
Vin = (Tdown / Tup) × Vref = 163.666 / 200.000 * 2V = 1,63666V


Visualizing the Process
The logic analyzer trace below shows the full conversion cycle. Note: since the analog front end and internal ADC share different grounds, the measurements appear offset by +5V. This is an artifact of differential grounding and trying to safe my oscilloscope, not an error.

Yellow (TP2105): Input signal for the ADC

Here, the input voltage was about 1.63 V. The waveforms clearly show the capacitor voltage ramping up linearly, followed by a discharge as it ramps down under a controlled current.


A Second Example
To verify the behavior, I captured another cycle with a smaller input voltage (≈600 mV). As expected, the capacitor discharges in less time, and the total count during ramp-down is correspondingly smaller. The measurement luckily tracks closely with theory, confirming the dual-slope ADC’s reliability, decades after it was built.
